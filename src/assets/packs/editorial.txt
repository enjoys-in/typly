There is a quiet argument running through every debate about employment in India, and it is rarely stated plainly: the country is not short of candidates, it is short of the narrow, verifiable skills that public recruitment is built to measure. A typing test is the clearest example. It is not a proxy for intelligence, nor for education, nor for suitability to a clerical post in any broad sense. It measures one thing, it measures it precisely, and it is therefore almost impossible to argue with.

That precision is what makes it worth taking seriously. A candidate who types thirty-two words a minute and needs thirty-five does not have a vague problem to be solved by working harder in general. They have a specific deficit of three words a minute, which can be measured today, measured again next week, and closed with practice that is directed rather than diffuse. Very few obstacles in a young person's life are so tractable.

The difficulty is that most preparation ignores this. Hours are spent on material that is either far too easy, which builds confidence and nothing else, or far too hard, which builds only frustration. Neither moves the number. What moves the number is honest measurement, repeated often, against the standard the examination itself will apply — and the willingness to read the result even when it is disappointing.

None of this argues that speed alone should decide who gets a government job. It argues something narrower and more useful: that where a requirement is stated as a number, treating it as a number is the fastest way past it.
